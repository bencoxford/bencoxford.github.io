I Gave My AI Assistant Access to My Entire Life. Here's What Happened

This morning, I asked my AI assistant to fix a broken Gmail permission. It diagnosed the problem, walked me through re-authenticating, noticed the Gmail API wasn't enabled in my Google Cloud project, and sent me the exact link to fix it. Then it pulled my heart rate, stress levels, and Body Battery from my Garmin watch - all before 11am. This isn't ChatGPT.

It doesn't forget me the moment I close the tab. It knows my name, my timezone, my Google account, and now my resting heart rate. It lives on my phone via WhatsApp. And I set the whole thing up in a single Saturday morning.

Here's what actually happened - and what it feels like to give an AI real access to your life.

The Problem With AI Assistants Today

Most AI assistants are goldfish - they have a memory problem. You open a tab, type a question, get an answer, close the tab. Tomorrow you open a new tab and it has no idea who you are. You're a stranger again.

Every single time. It's a fundamental ceiling if you want something that actually assists you - the way a real assistant would. One that knows your schedule, remembers what you talked about last week, notices when something's off. ChatGPT doesn't know your name unless you tell it.

It doesn't know you skipped breakfast, that you have a meeting in an hour, or that your stress levels have been quietly creeping up all week. It's knowledgeable but blind. Capable but disconnected. The assistants we actually want - the ones from films, the ones we imagined when AI first became a thing - aren't just smart.

They're present. They're woven into your life. They notice things. They remember things.

They act, not just advise. Sure, an Iron Man suit would be cool, but how cool would it be to have your own J.A.R.V.I.S or F.R.I.D.A.Y? That's the gap I went looking to close. And one Saturday morning, I think I found a way!

What "Access to Your Life" Actually Means

Would you hand over the keys to your life? When I say I gave my AI assistant access to my life, I don't mean I let it read a few documents. I mean I handed it the keys. Gmail.

My Garmin health data. WhatsApp. A persistent memory that survives between conversations. The ability to run commands, check things, fix things - not just talk about them.

Let me be specific, because the details matter.

Email

The first thing I connected was Gmail. Not in a "paste this email into the chat" way - in a "here are my OAuth credentials, go read my inbox" way. It can search my emails, draft replies, send messages on my behalf. It knows what I've received today.

It can flag things that matter. Getting there wasn't completely smooth - the Gmail API wasn't enabled in my Google Cloud project, which meant the first auth attempt failed silently. The assistant diagnosed it, found the exact error, and sent me the link to fix it. That moment felt different.

It wasn't just answering a question. It was solving a problem.

Health Data

This one surprised me with how deep it goes. Using Garmin's API, the assistant can pull my resting heart rate, HRV, stress levels, Body Battery, steps, sleep stages, VO2 max - a real-time picture of how my body is doing. Not summaries. Not weekly reports.

Actual data, on demand. This morning my peak stress level was 88. I didn't know that until the assistant told me. Now I'm curious what caused it - and that curiosity is the whole point. (I also went down a rabbit hole trying to connect Health Connect, Google's Android health data platform.

Short answer: it's locked to your phone, there's no web API, and it's a dead end for now. Sometimes the honest answer is "not yet".)

Always a Message Away

The thing that makes it feel real rather than theoretical is WhatsApp. The assistant lives there. I don't open a browser, navigate to a tool, start a new session. I just message it - the same way I'd message a person.

It replies. It remembers. It picks up where we left off. That shift from "tool I visit" to "presence I talk to" is subtle but it changes everything about how you interact with it.

Persistent Memory

Unlike every other AI I've used, this one remembers me between sessions. Not because it has some magical long-term model - but because it writes things down. Notes, context, decisions, preferences. The next time I talk to it, it reads those notes and knows who I am.

It sounds simple. It changes everything.

What Surprised Me

I went into this expecting a clever automation. What I got felt like something more.

It actually remembers things

I know I mentioned this in the last section, but I did not want a goldfish. I wanted something with a memory. The first time I came back to a conversation and the assistant already knew my name, my timezone, what we'd talked about before - I had to pause. Every other AI tool I've used resets.

This one doesn't. It's a small thing technically. Psychologically it's enormous.

The moment it felt less like a tool

It happened during the Gmail setup. The API wasn't enabled, the auth failed, and before I'd even finished reading the error message the assistant had already identified the problem and dropped the exact link I needed to fix it. No back and forth. No "here are some things that might be wrong." Just: here's the issue, here's the fix.

That's not what tools do. Tools wait to be used. This felt like something that was paying attention.

It got things wrong too

Last night's sleep data came back null. The assistant was upfront about it - probably the watch wasn't worn, or the sync hadn't come through yet. No hallucinated sleep score, no confident guess. Just an honest "the data isn't there." That matters more than it sounds.

An assistant that makes things up to seem helpful is worse than useless. One that says "I don't know" when it doesn't know? That's one you can actually trust.

Voice - In Both Directions

One of the quieter additions that ended up feeling surprisingly natural: voice. The assistant can speak. Using a local text-to-speech engine running entirely on my Mac - no data sent anywhere, no cloud service - it can read things back to me in a warm, natural voice. Ask it for a summary and it doesn't just type one out; it can tell you one.

For longer content - a news digest, a story, a health summary - hearing it rather than reading it changes the experience in a way that's hard to articulate until you've tried it. It can also listen. Speech-to-text means I don't always have to type. Hands busy?

Walking around? Just talk. The transcription comes through and the assistant responds as normal. Neither of these is new technology.

But combined with everything else - the context, the memory, the real integrations - they tip the experience from "AI chatbot" into something that genuinely feels like a presence in your space rather than an app on your screen.

The Uncomfortable Bit

Let's not skip past this, because it deserves honest treatment. By the end of the morning, my AI assistant could read my emails, see my health data, message me on WhatsApp, and remember everything we'd discussed. That's a lot of access. And if you're not at least a little uncomfortable with that, you probably haven't thought about it hard enough.

What it can see

It can see my inbox. It knows my resting heart rate and stress levels. It knows my name, my timezone, where I'm living right now, and what I've been working on. If I asked it to, it could read my calendar, search my Drive, and pull my contacts.

That's not a hypothetical threat surface. That's real access to real things. So why did I do it? Because the alternative - an AI that can only see what you paste into a chat box - isn't actually useful for the things that matter.

The whole value proposition of a personal assistant is that it knows your situation. A doctor who can only tell you what you tell them isn't as good as one who can actually run tests. The trade-off is real. So is the upside.

What made me comfortable enough

A few things. The assistant runs on my own machine in a container- it's not a third-party SaaS storing my data on someone else's servers. The credentials are stored in my Mac's Keychain, not in a plain text file. And critically, I can see exactly what it's doing.

There's no black box. When it accesses my Gmail or pulls my Garmin data, I can trace every step. Transparency and control are the difference between trust and blind faith. I'm not doing blind faith.

The boundary that matters most

The one I care about: it doesn't send anything externally without checking first. It can read freely. Writing, sending, posting - those require my sign-off. That asymmetry feels right.

Information in is low risk. Information out is where you have to be careful. If you're thinking about doing this yourself, that's the line I'd recommend drawing first.

A note on the model underneath

The brain powering all of this is both Google's Gemma 4 model and Claude, Anthropic's AI model. One thing worth knowing - and this actually made me more comfortable, not less - is that Anthropic only retains conversation data for 30 days via the API (I opted out to send my data for training). It's not building a permanent profile of you on their servers. The memory that persists is the memory I control, in files on my own machine.

Anthropic processes the conversation, then it's gone on their end. The long-term context lives with me. As for Gemma 4, this runs locally on my machine and runs the simple tasks like heartbeats as to not use up tokens daily and keep costs low. That's a meaningful distinction.

You're not feeding a corporation an ever-growing dossier on your life. You're using a model as a reasoning engine, with your own files as the memory layer. The intelligence is theirs; the data is yours.

Where this Goes Next

The next step is a daily health summary - every morning, a digest of last night's sleep, current Body Battery, HRV trend, stress levels. Not a dashboard I have to go open. Just a message that shows up, like a check-in from something that's paying attention. After that: proactive nudges.

Calendar conflicts flagged before they become problems. Emails that actually need a response surfaced rather than buried. Health patterns noticed over weeks, not just days. The vision isn't an assistant that answers questions.

It's one that spots things before you think to ask.

The bigger picture

We've spent years being told AI will change everything, and mostly it's changed how we google things. This felt different. Not because it's magic - it's not - but because for the first time the AI was in my life rather than adjacent to it. That's the shift.

And once you feel it, the old way of doing things feels oddly flat.

The Stack

Everything runs on OpenClaw - an open-source AI assistant framework that handles the plumbing: memory, integrations, scheduling, and messaging. The model underneath is both Google's Gemma 4 and Claude by Anthropic. Garmin data comes via the garminconnect Python library; although, I have switched to Google's Health Connect. Gmail through the gog CLI.

WhatsApp is handled natively by OpenClaw's gateway. Credentials live in macOS Keychain. Text-to-speech runs locally via Kokoro TTS - nothing leaves the machine. I plan to write another article walking through the setup, covering security principles and considerations.

The whole thing runs on my Mac mini. No cloud subscriptions, no third-party data stores. Just a framework, a model API, and a Saturday morning.
