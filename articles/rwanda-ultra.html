RUNNING / ULTRAMARATHON

Running Ultra X Rwanda:
Heat, Ginger Biscuits, and 110km of Lessons

By Ben Coxford

Jump to gallery ↓

In January 2026, I ran Ultra X 2026 Rwanda Marathon—a 110km ultramarathon across the hills and villages of Rwanda. What I thought would be my toughest physical challenge became a masterclass in what happens when you don't respect your nutrition, and how a small decision (eating ginger biscuits at a checkpoint) can change the course of a race.

The Race Setup

Ultra X Rwanda takes place near Volcanoes National Park—home to the famous mountain gorillas—and straddles two stunning twin lakes: Lake Ruhondo and Lake Burera. The course is a sensory journey over gentle hills and valleys, through traditional coffee and banana fields with lush, jungle-like vegetation. Throughout the race, you're treated to the incredible backdrop of Rwanda's five iconic volcanoes: Mt Muhabura, Mt Sabyinyo, Mt Karisimbi, Mt Bisoke, and Mt Gahinga.

The race splits into two stages. Saturday's stage is 60km starting at 06:30 from the campsite near Kinigi. Sunday's stage is 50km starting at 07:00, bringing runners back to the Lake Ruhondo campsite where we'd slept the night before. Checkpoints are positioned approximately 12km apart, providing water, shelter, and medical support.

We camped by Lake Ruhondo the night before racing, and the landscape alone was worth the journey. The views were something else—snow-capped volcanoes across the water at dawn, forest canopy that felt almost prehistoric in places. Two days before the race, our team did a kit test run. It rained that day, which was funny in the moment but turned out to be important practice for what was coming.

Day One: The Heat and the Mistake

The first day was where it all went wrong. Not catastrophically wrong—I didn't DNF—but wrong enough to teach me something I should have already known.

I got heat sickness somewhere around checkpoint 4. The problem was simple: I didn't do my nutrition properly. I was running strong, felt good, and convinced myself that I didn't need to eat as much as I'd planned. By checkpoint 4, my body was angry about that decision. I was sick, feeling rough, and completely unprepared for what to do next.

I sat down, took a break, and tried to recover. Someone gave me a banana—small, sweet, just what I needed in the moment. I was grateful for it, but I was still running on fumes.

Then someone suggested ginger biscuits.

I'll be honest—I was skeptical. Ginger biscuits? Really? But at that point, I was willing to try anything. And they worked. Something about the ginger, the carbs, and just sitting still for a moment while I ate them slowly turned it around. Within 30 minutes of finishing those biscuits, I felt human again.

I left checkpoint 4 and continued running.

The Village and the Laughter

About 500 meters down the road after checkpoint 4, I was walking through a populated village, still recovering from the heat sickness. And I threw up on the side of the road.

It was not a dignified moment.

But here's what I remember most: the Rwandan kids who saw it thought it was hilarious. They were laughing, pointing, amused by this sweaty, struggling runner who'd just been sick on their street. I was too tired to be embarrassed. I just kept walking, and they kept laughing. Looking back, I laughed at myself too—there's something absurd about the whole situation. It wasn't anger at my nutrition failure, just frustration at myself for letting it get to that point.

That moment stuck with me. There's something oddly liberating about being that vulnerable in front of strangers, and having them respond with laughter instead of judgment. It broke something in me—the part that was worried about looking tough or being in control. After that, I just focused on moving forward.

What I Learned

Nutrition is not optional. I know this intellectually, but this race proved it viscerally. The moment I stopped eating regularly was the moment my body started to fail. There's no amount of fitness or mental toughness that overrides the basic chemistry of carbs, electrolytes, and water.

Sometimes the small things fix everything. Ginger biscuits aren't fancy race nutrition. They're not designed by sports scientists. But they worked because they were simple, they were something I could actually stomach, and they gave me what I needed in that moment.

Resilience isn't about never struggling. It's about what happens after you do. I got heat sickness, I recovered, I kept going. That's it. I didn't need to be superhuman. I just needed to eat some biscuits and get back on my feet.

Community is everywhere, even on random Rwandan roads. I had an incredible support crew who met me at every checkpoint (every 10km) with water, food, and encouragement. But what surprised me most was the Rwandan people along the route—they didn't need to be there, yet they cheered us on. And when I failed on the side of the road in front of a village, they still laughed with me. That matters.

The Bigger Picture

Ultra X Rwanda was beautiful and hard and humbling. The landscape is stunning—every checkpoint opened up to a new view of the valley, the lakes, the forests. The organization was solid. The support from the crew was excellent. And the Rwandan people along the route were wonderful, cheering us on even as we struggled.

But the race itself taught me that endurance isn't about pushing through. It's about respecting the basics: fuel your body, drink water, rest when you need to. It's about knowing that sometimes you'll fail at the side of the road in front of an audience, and that's okay.

I finished the 110km. It took 25 hours, 46 minutes, and 35 seconds, and I came in 23rd overall—a solid finish given what happened on day one. I went through hell on Saturday, but I finished strong on Sunday. And I'd do it again—just with better nutrition planning, and probably with ginger biscuits packed from the start.

Ultra X Rwanda is a UTMB® indexed race, recognized globally for its quality. If you're looking for a stunning ultramarathon in an unforgettable landscape with incredible support, I can't recommend it highly enough.

Tips for Running Ultra X Rwanda

Navigation and Communication — You'll have good 4G coverage throughout the race, which is helpful. The organizers provide a GPX file you can load directly into your Garmin watch, so you know exactly where you're going. Flag markers are placed across the course, but be aware: locals tend to take them as souvenirs. Having the GPS backup is essential. Keep important numbers, insurance info, and emergency contacts written down or accessible on your phone.

Gear and Kit Investment — Invest in good running shoes. I wore the North Face IKB Vectiv 3 Blue trainers, and they were incredible. Get proper socks—Twin Skin socks, specifically. I cannot stress this enough. After 110km through varying terrain, moisture, and heat, blisters are inevitable without the right gear. Twin Skin socks kept my feet in decent shape when they could have been destroyed.

Hydration and Nutrition — Hydrate, hydrate, hydrate. I carried a 1.5L tank in my trail running pack, which allowed me to manage water between checkpoints. For food, bring snacks and treats you actually love. Yes, bring your electrolytes and energy gels. Trail mix is excellent. But also bring your favorite sweets or treats for morale. Pack MRE-style meals (the kind where you add water)—they're convenient and relatively lightweight. Bring biscuits and additional treats. Salt, pepper, and hot sauce sachets make a huge difference when you're eating bland food at checkpoints.

Skip energy bars if you're running in hot weather—mine melted into an unusable gooy mess. The rehydrated scrambled eggs at camp were absolutely disgusting, but I forced them down anyway. Lesson learned: know what you can stomach when you're exhausted.

Health and Protection — Pack sunscreen and anti-chafe products. These are non-negotiable. Bring a proper first-aid kit that includes blister treatment—you'll be grateful you did. Head torch is essential. We arrived back quite late on day two, running through the wilderness in darkness. A good headlamp is the difference between feeling safe and feeling lost.

Battery and Power — Bring battery packs to charge your devices at camp. This is very useful for overnight charging. I brought a spare Motorola Power phone with incredible battery life, which was essential for music and podcasts during the long stretches between checkpoints.

Camping — The campsite is good. You get access to showers, but arrive early—they get cold as the day goes on and they run out of hot water. You make your own food at camp, so plan accordingly. Bring ingredients, instant meals, or pre-prepared options. Here's a key advantage: you're allowed a 10kg bag to transport between camps. This means you can leave your meals and supplies at camp and don't need to carry everything on the trail. Pack your meals and heavier items in this bag, and it'll be waiting for you at the next camp. This makes the weight on your back much more manageable during the race itself.

Running Partners — Run with friends if you can. I ran the entire race with my friend Talia, and having someone to push through the tough moments with made all the difference. We kept each other going when it would have been easy to walk. Our friend Jahin was supposed to join us for the whole race too. He stuck with us through the first couple of kilometers, but pretty quickly found his own rhythm and pace—which was exactly right for him. We all knew he'd end up running his own race (it was the smart move), and we were genuinely happy for him to do his thing. The point is: having people there, whether you're running together or apart, changes the experience.

What's Next?

Ultra X Rwanda was incredible, but it's not the end. I've already committed to the next challenge: a 220km ultramarathon in Kenya in May 2027. For this race, I'm raising £2,500 for Mind UK—continuing the mission to support mental health awareness and services. Every mile, every checkpoint, every moment of struggle is for a cause bigger than the finish line.

If you want to support the challenge or learn more, you can find my fundraising page on JustGiving. The miles continue.

Beyond the Finish Line: Gorilla Trekking

After the race, we stayed one more night at the camp to recover. The food was incredible—rice, grilled chicken, and fresh vegetables. The next day, we headed to Kigali, Rwanda's capital, for a completely different kind of adventure.

We woke up at 3am to do gorilla trekking in Volcanoes National Park. The permit alone was $800, with an additional $400 for our guide to split between us. We traveled in a jeep up into the mountains. Our guide for the trek was fantastic—he was wearing a Sainsbury's supermarket jumper, which was funny because he had no idea what it was or where it came from. That's the kind of moment that sticks with you.

The trek itself took about an hour through dense forest. We had machete-wielding guides clearing the way, and sherpas helping us navigate the steep, muddy sections (you tip them for their support—they're invaluable on those brutal slopes). The money we paid goes directly to conserving the wildlife and protecting their habitat, which made the experience feel like more than just tourism.

And then we saw them. The gorillas. There were baby gorillas play-fighting each other, which was magical to witness. Adult gorillas were nearby, going about their day, unbothered by our presence. It rained a bit on the way back down, but it didn't matter. The trek was worth every penny and every muddy step.

After that, we visited the Ellen DeGeneres Campus of the Dian Fossey Gorilla Fund—a research and conservation facility dedicated to protecting these incredible animals. It was humbling to learn about the work being done. We finished the day with an incredible, fancy meal at a restaurant on the way back to Kigali, celebrating not just the ultra, but the whole adventure.
